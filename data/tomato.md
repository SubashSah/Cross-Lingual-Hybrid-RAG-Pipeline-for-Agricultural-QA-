​# Tomato Cultivation in Nepal: A Practical Production Guide

## 1. Introduction

Tomato (_Solanum lycopersicum_, formerly _Lycopersicon esculentum_) is one of Nepal’s most important commercial vegetable crops. It is grown for household consumption, local markets, long-distance trade, hotels, restaurants, and processing. Tomatoes are eaten fresh in salads and cooked foods and are also processed into sauce, ketchup, puree, juice, and pickles.

Tomato is naturally a warm-season crop, but Nepal’s wide variation in altitude and climate allows production during different periods of the year. In the Terai, tomato is mainly grown during the cooler months. In the hills, it can be grown during spring, summer, and the rainy season, particularly under plastic houses. With suitable variety selection, nursery management, irrigation, plant protection, and protected cultivation, farmers can produce tomatoes almost throughout the year.

Tomato fruits contain vitamins, minerals, organic acids, and antioxidant compounds such as lycopene. Market quality depends mainly on fruit size, colour, firmness, taste, shelf life, and freedom from disease and physical damage.

---

## 2. Climate Requirements

Tomato performs best under warm, moderately dry conditions with good sunlight.

- The most suitable temperature for growth is generally around **20–25°C**.
- Fruit setting is usually best when daytime temperature is about **25–28°C** and night temperature remains around **13–21°C**.
- Temperatures below about **15–16°C** may slow growth and reduce fruit setting.
- Very high temperatures, especially above **30–35°C**, can dry pollen, cause flower drop, reduce fruit setting, and produce poorly shaped fruits.c
- Tomato does not tolerate frost.
- Excessive rainfall, waterlogging, and prolonged high humidity increase the risk of bacterial wilt, late blight, damping-off, leaf diseases, and fruit cracking.
- Low light inside a dense or poorly designed plastic house reduces flowering, fruit development, and yield.

Because temperature varies with altitude, the same variety and planting date are not suitable for every location. Farmers should select the production season according to the local climate and market window.

## 4. Types of Tomato Plants

Tomato varieties are commonly grouped according to their growth habit.

### 4.1 Determinate Types

Determinate plants are short, compact, and stop growing after producing a limited number of flower clusters. Most fruits mature within a relatively short period.

They are suitable for:

- Open-field cultivation
- Kitchen gardens
- Areas where staking materials are limited
- Farmers who want concentrated harvesting
- Processing and bulk marketing

Determinate types generally require less pruning and staking than tall varieties.

### 4.2 Semi-Determinate Types

Semi-determinate varieties have growth characteristics between determinate and indeterminate types. They grow taller than bush varieties but remain more compact than fully indeterminate plants.

They are suitable for:

- Open fields with support
- Plastic houses with moderate training
- Farmers seeking a longer harvest period than determinate varieties

### 4.3 Indeterminate Types

Indeterminate plants continue to grow, flower, and produce fruits for a long period when temperature and crop management remain favourable.

They are suitable for:

- Commercial plastic-house cultivation
- Off-season production
- Long harvesting periods
- Regular supply to markets

These varieties require strong staking, pruning, training, balanced nutrition, and regular pest and disease monitoring.

---

## 5. Variety Selection

Variety selection should be based on location, season, market demand, production system, disease pressure, fruit firmness, and expected selling distance.

Important selection criteria include:

- Adaptation to local temperature and altitude
- Resistance or tolerance to bacterial wilt, leaf curl virus, root-knot nematode, and other local problems
- Determinate or indeterminate growth habit
- Fruit size, shape, colour, and firmness
- Shelf life and transportability
- Suitability for open field or plastic house
- Consumer preference
- Availability of genuine seed

**Srijana** is an important tomato hybrid developed and promoted in Nepal and is widely associated with commercial and plastic-house production. Other varieties and hybrids may perform well in particular locations, but farmers should use seed from reliable suppliers and choose varieties recommended by local agriculture offices, research stations, or experienced producers in similar conditions.

A variety that performs well in the Terai winter may not perform equally well in a mid-hill plastic house during the rainy season.

---

## 6. Soil and Site Selection

Tomato grows in several soil types, but the best production is usually obtained from fertile, well-drained loam or sandy-loam soil rich in organic matter.

### Suitable soil conditions

- Soil pH: approximately **6.0–7.0**
- Good drainage
- Good moisture-holding capacity
- High organic matter
- No hardpan near the root zone
- Access to clean irrigation water
- Good sunlight and air movement

Low-lying or waterlogged land should be avoided. Raised beds are especially useful during the rainy season and in plastic houses.

Tomato, potato, chilli, eggplant, and other crops in the Solanaceae family share several diseases and pests. Therefore, tomato should not be planted repeatedly in the same field. Crop rotation with maize, wheat, rice, legumes, cucurbits, or other unrelated crops helps reduce soil-borne diseases and nematodes.

---

## 7. Nursery Management

Healthy seedlings are the foundation of a productive tomato crop. Weak or infected seedlings often result in poor crop establishment and early disease outbreaks.

### 7.1 Nursery Site

Select a nursery site that:

- Has not recently grown tomato, potato, chilli, or eggplant
- Has good drainage
- Receives sufficient sunlight
- Is close to clean water
- Can be protected from heavy rain, frost, and strong wind

### 7.2 Nursery Bed Preparation

A practical raised nursery bed may be:

- About **1 metre wide**
- About **15 cm high**
- Of convenient length

For approximately one ropani of field, around **3–6 square metres** of nursery area may be sufficient depending on the seedling system and spacing. Mix well-decomposed compost into the upper soil before sowing.

Nursery soil can be treated through solarization during hot months. Moist soil is covered tightly with transparent plastic for several weeks so that heat reduces soil-borne pathogens, weed seeds, and some pests.

Seedling trays filled with clean growing media are preferable where available because they reduce root damage and soil-borne infection.

### 7.3 Seed Rate and Sowing

Seed requirement depends on germination percentage, variety, seed size, and whether seedlings are raised in beds or trays. A practical range for one ropani is approximately **7–10 grams of quality seed**, although some systems may use less hybrid seed.

In nursery beds:

- Make shallow rows about 5–7.5 cm apart.
- Sow seeds thinly rather than densely.
- Cover with a fine layer of soil or compost.
- Apply light irrigation using a rose can.
- Cover temporarily with dry grass or straw until germination.
- Remove the cover after seedlings emerge.

Dense sowing, excessive irrigation, poor ventilation, and contaminated soil encourage damping-off.

### 7.4 Seedling Care

- Maintain light and regular moisture, but do not flood the nursery.
- Protect summer nurseries from heavy rain and strong sunlight using a simple shade or rain shelter.
- Protect winter nurseries from cold using a low plastic tunnel.
- Remove diseased seedlings immediately.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen, which produces weak and elongated seedlings.
- Begin hardening seedlings 5–7 days before transplanting by slightly reducing water and gradually exposing them to field conditions.

Seedlings are usually ready for transplanting at **25–30 days**, when they have about **4–5 true leaves**, a strong stem, and a healthy root system.

---

## 8. Land Preparation and Bed Formation

Plough or dig the field two to three times and break large soil clods. Remove weeds and previous crop residues. Incorporate well-decomposed farmyard manure or compost during land preparation.

Raised beds improve drainage, root aeration, irrigation control, and crop management. They are particularly important in:

- Rainy-season production
- Heavy soils
- Plastic houses
- Fields with previous waterlogging problems

Beds should be arranged so that workers can prune, spray, harvest, and inspect plants without stepping near the root zone.

---

## 9. Manure and Fertilizer Management

Tomato requires a steady and balanced supply of nutrients. Fertilizer should ideally be based on a soil test, expected yield, variety, production system, and previous crop history.

A NARC best-management recommendation for commercial tomato production per ropani includes approximately:

- **16 kg DAP**
- **8.3 kg potash**
- **15 kg urea**, divided into two applications

DAP and potash are generally applied during planting or final land preparation. Urea is divided, with one application around 20 days after transplanting and another near fruit-setting.

However, fertilizer rates should not be copied blindly. Plastic-house crops with a long harvesting period, highly fertile soils, drip fertigation, and low-yield open-field crops have different nutrient requirements.

### Good nutrient-management practices

- Apply well-decomposed farmyard manure or compost before transplanting.
- Use soil-test recommendations whenever available.
- Avoid excessive urea, which causes soft growth, delayed fruiting, more insect attack, and greater disease risk.
- Split nitrogen fertilizer rather than applying it all at once.
- Maintain adequate potassium for fruit firmness, colour, taste, and shelf life.
- Maintain calcium and regular moisture to reduce blossom-end rot.
- Correct boron or zinc deficiency only after identifying a real need.
- Do not place concentrated fertilizer directly against the roots.
- Under drip irrigation, soluble fertilizer may be applied in small, frequent doses through fertigation.

Organic manure should be fully decomposed. Fresh manure may damage roots, introduce weed seeds, and increase disease problems.

---

## 10. Transplanting and Spacing

Transplant in the late afternoon, evening, or on a cloudy day to reduce stress. Irrigate the nursery before lifting seedlings so that roots remain intact.

After transplanting:

- Press soil gently around the root zone.
- Irrigate immediately.
- Replace dead or weak seedlings within 3–4 days.

Recommended spacing depends on plant type and training method.

### General spacing guide

- **Tall or indeterminate varieties:** about **75 cm between rows and 60 cm between plants**
- **Short or determinate varieties:** about **60 cm between rows and 40–50 cm between plants**
- A **60 × 60 cm** spacing may also be used in some drip-irrigated systems.

Closer spacing increases plant population but may reduce air movement and increase disease. Wider spacing improves ventilation but may reduce efficient land use. Plastic-house spacing should match the pruning and training system.

---

## 11. Irrigation and Drainage

Tomato requires consistent soil moisture throughout growth, especially during flowering, fruit setting, and fruit enlargement.

### Irrigation principles

- Irrigate immediately after transplanting.
- During hot weather, irrigation may be needed every **3–5 days** depending on soil and crop stage.
- During cool weather, the interval may extend to **10–15 days**.
- Drip irrigation is the preferred method for plastic houses and dry-season production.
- Avoid wetting the foliage repeatedly, especially in the evening.
- Maintain drainage during the rainy season.
- Do not allow severe drying followed by heavy irrigation, as this encourages fruit cracking.
- Avoid over-irrigation, which reduces oxygen in the root zone and increases root rot, wilt, and nutrient loss.

Actual irrigation frequency should be decided by soil moisture rather than a fixed calendar.

---

## 12. Mulching

Mulching is highly useful in tomato cultivation.

Organic mulch such as straw can:

- Conserve soil moisture
- Suppress weeds
- Reduce soil splashing onto leaves and fruits
- Moderate soil temperature
- Reduce fruit contact with soil

Plastic mulch can provide stronger weed control and moisture conservation but must be properly installed and responsibly collected and disposed of after use.

Mulch should not be placed tightly against the stem because constant moisture around the stem can encourage disease.

---

## 13. Staking, Training, and Pruning

### 13.1 Staking

Staking keeps plants and fruits above the ground, improves air movement, makes spraying and harvesting easier, and reduces fruit rotting.

Materials may include:

- Bamboo stakes
- Wooden stakes
- Vertical strings
- Trellis wire
- Plastic twine designed for crop support

Indeterminate tomatoes require strong support. Staking is especially important during the rainy season and inside plastic houses.

### 13.2 Training

Plants may be trained to one or two main stems. A one-stem system allows closer spacing and better ventilation but requires more labour. A two-stem system produces a larger canopy and may suit wider spacing.

### 13.3 Pruning

Pruning is mainly practised in indeterminate and some semi-determinate varieties.

Remove:

- Unwanted side shoots
- Leaves touching the soil
- Yellow, damaged, or diseased leaves
- Excessive vegetative growth
- Shoots below the selected main branches

Prune when shoots are small. Large wounds increase disease risk. Use clean hands or disinfected tools and avoid pruning when plants are wet.

Do not over-prune. Excessive leaf removal exposes fruits to sunscald and reduces photosynthesis.

---

## 14. Off-Season Tomato Production under Plastic Houses

Off-season production allows farmers to supply tomatoes when open-field production is low and market prices may be higher. It is especially important in the mid-hills during the rainy season.

### 14.1 Purpose of a Plastic House

A plastic house helps:

- Protect foliage and flowers from heavy rainfall
- Reduce rain-splash disease spread
- Control irrigation
- Extend the production season
- Improve fruit quality
- Support staking and training

A plastic house does not automatically prevent disease. Poor ventilation, excessive humidity, continuous tomato cultivation, and poor sanitation can create serious bacterial wilt, late blight, powdery mildew, whitefly, and nematode problems.

### 14.2 Site and Structure

Select a site with:

- Full sunlight
- Good drainage
- Access to water
- Protection from strong winds
- Enough space for ventilation and drainage channels

Locally available bamboo can be used for low-cost structures, while stronger commercial houses may use metal frames. Use agricultural-grade ultraviolet-stabilized plastic rather than thin household plastic. The design should allow rainwater to drain away and side ventilation to remain open during hot weather.

### 14.3 Cropping Schedule

In the mid-hills, seedlings established around Chait may provide harvest from Jestha onward, while a second crop transplanted around Shrawan may produce during Asoj–Mangsir. Actual timing must be adjusted for altitude, temperature, and market conditions.

Continuous tomato cropping inside the same house should be avoided. Rotate with non-solanaceous crops and use sanitation, solarization, grafted plants, or resistant rootstocks where bacterial wilt and root-knot nematode are serious.

---

## 15. Weed Management

Weeds compete for nutrients, water, and light and can host insects and diseases.

Recommended practices include:

- Clean field preparation
- Shallow hoeing before weeds become large
- Mulching
- Hand weeding around plants
- Keeping paths and plastic-house borders clean
- Earthing up after early weeding where appropriate

Deep hoeing near mature plants can damage roots. Herbicides should only be used when registered for tomato and according to the approved label and technical advice.

---

## 16. Pollination and Fruit Setting

Tomato is mainly self-pollinated, but flower vibration helps release pollen. In open fields, wind and insect movement usually assist pollination. In closed plastic houses, poor ventilation and extreme temperature can reduce fruit setting.

To improve fruit setting:

- Maintain good side ventilation.
- Avoid excessive heat and humidity.
- Do not overapply nitrogen.
- Maintain regular irrigation.
- Gently shake supporting wires or flower clusters when natural vibration is limited.
- Protect pollinating insects by avoiding unnecessary insecticide use during flowering.

Flower drop can result from very high or low temperature, water stress, nutrient imbalance, disease, or poor light.

---

## 17. Integrated Pest and Disease Management

The best approach is prevention, regular monitoring, sanitation, resistant varieties, crop rotation, and need-based treatment.

### 17.1 General Prevention

- Use healthy seed and seedlings.
- Raise nurseries in clean or treated soil.
- Rotate with non-solanaceous crops.
- Remove and destroy infected plants and fruits.
- Maintain good drainage and ventilation.
- Avoid excessive plant density and nitrogen.
- Disinfect pruning tools.
- Control weeds around the field.
- Use sticky traps and pheromone traps for monitoring.
- Avoid repeated use of the same pesticide group.
- Use only pesticides registered for tomato in Nepal and follow the label, waiting period, protective equipment, and local technical advice.

### 17.2 Damping-Off

**Symptoms:** Seedlings rot near the soil surface, fall over, and die.

**Favouring conditions:** Dense sowing, contaminated soil, high moisture, poor drainage, and poor ventilation.

**Management:**

- Use clean nursery media.
- Avoid excessive watering.
- Sow seed thinly.
- Solarize nursery soil where possible.
- Remove affected seedlings.
- Use approved biological or fungicidal seed and nursery treatments only when necessary.

### 17.3 Bacterial Wilt

**Symptoms:** Plants wilt suddenly while leaves may initially remain green. Cutting the lower stem and placing it in clean water may show a white bacterial stream.

**Management:**

- Uproot infected plants with roots and remove them from the field.
- Do not move contaminated soil or irrigation water.
- Rotate for several years with non-solanaceous crops.
- Use tolerant varieties or grafted plants on resistant rootstocks.
- Improve drainage.
- Avoid planting seedlings from infected nurseries.

Bacterial wilt is difficult to cure after infection, so prevention is essential.

### 17.4 Early Blight

**Symptoms:** Brown spots with concentric rings, usually beginning on older leaves. Severe infection causes leaf drying and may affect stems and fruits.

**Management:**

- Remove infected crop residue.
- Maintain balanced nutrition.
- Avoid overhead irrigation.
- Improve spacing and ventilation.
- Apply a registered fungicide only when disease risk or symptoms justify it.

### 17.5 Late Blight

**Symptoms:** Rapidly expanding brown or dark lesions on leaves, stems, and fruits, often during cool, humid, or rainy conditions. Whitish growth may appear on the lower leaf surface.

**Management:**

- Inspect frequently during cloudy and wet weather.
- Remove heavily infected material.
- Ventilate plastic houses.
- Avoid prolonged leaf wetness.
- Use preventive or curative fungicides registered for tomato according to local recommendations.

Late blight can spread quickly, so early detection is critical.

### 17.6 Fusarium Wilt

**Symptoms:** Yellowing starts on lower leaves, sometimes on one side of the plant, followed by wilting. The vascular tissue may turn brown.

**Management:**

- Use resistant varieties.
- Rotate crops.
- Improve soil health and drainage.
- Avoid moving infected soil.
- Use grafted seedlings where the problem is persistent.

### 17.7 Leaf Curl Virus

**Symptoms:** Upward or downward curling, reduced leaf size, stunting, excessive branching, flower drop, and poor fruiting.

**Spread:** Mainly by whiteflies.

**Management:**

- Use healthy seedlings.
- Remove infected plants early.
- Control weeds that host whiteflies.
- Use insect-proof nursery netting where possible.
- Install yellow sticky traps.
- Manage whiteflies with integrated methods.
- Avoid unnecessary movement from infected to healthy plots.

### 17.8 Root-Knot Nematode

**Symptoms:** Swollen knots or galls on roots, weak growth, yellowing, and wilting despite adequate water.

**Management:**

- Use clean nursery soil.
- Solarize soil.
- Rotate with non-host crops.
- Incorporate well-decomposed organic matter.
- Remove infected roots after harvest.
- Use resistant rootstocks and grafted seedlings in heavily affected plastic houses.

### 17.9 Fruit Borer

**Symptoms:** Caterpillars feed on flowers and bore into fruits, leaving holes and waste material.

**Management:**

- Collect and destroy damaged fruits.
- Monitor with pheromone traps.
- Encourage natural enemies.
- Grow trap crops such as marigold where locally recommended.
- Apply a selective registered insecticide only when pest numbers justify treatment.

### 17.10 Whitefly

**Damage:** Adults and nymphs suck sap and transmit leaf curl virus.

**Management:**

- Raise seedlings under insect-protective netting.
- Use yellow sticky traps.
- Remove weeds and infected plants.
- Avoid excessive insecticide use that kills natural enemies.
- Use approved biological or chemical products only when monitoring shows a need.

## 18. Important Physiological Disorders

### 18.1 Blossom-End Rot

A dark, sunken patch develops at the blossom end of the fruit. It is associated with poor calcium movement into the fruit, usually caused by irregular soil moisture, root damage, high salt concentration, or excessive vegetative growth.

**Management:**

- Maintain uniform soil moisture.
- Improve drainage and root health.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen.
- Maintain suitable soil pH and calcium availability.
- Do not depend only on foliar calcium sprays without correcting irrigation and root problems.

### 18.2 Fruit Cracking

Cracks may radiate from the stem end or form rings around the fruit shoulder.

**Causes:**

- Dry soil followed by heavy rain or irrigation
- Rapid fruit growth
- Large temperature fluctuations
- Variety susceptibility
- Nutrient imbalance

**Management:**

- Irrigate regularly.
- Mulch the soil.
- Use tolerant varieties.
- Harvest at the correct stage before heavy rain when possible.

### 18.3 Puffiness or Hollow Fruit

Affected fruits are light, angular, or hollow inside.

**Causes:**

- Poor pollination
- High or low temperature
- Nutrient imbalance
- Embryo abortion
- Poor fruit development

**Management:** Maintain suitable temperature, balanced nutrition, good pollination, and regular irrigation.

### 18.4 Sunscald

Exposed fruits develop pale, yellow, or burnt patches.

**Management:** Maintain enough healthy foliage, avoid excessive pruning, and reduce sudden exposure of fruits to intense sunlight.

### 18.5 Uneven or Blotchy Ripening

Fruits ripen unevenly and may retain green or yellow patches.

**Management:** Maintain balanced potassium and nitrogen, avoid heat stress, manage diseases, and harvest at an appropriate maturity stage.

## 19. Harvesting

Harvesting stage depends on market distance and intended use.

### Common maturity stages

1. **Immature green:** Fruit is not fully developed; unsuitable for normal harvest.
2. **Mature green:** Fruit is fully developed but still green; suitable for distant transport.
3. **Breaker stage:** The first visible colour change appears.
4. **Pink stage:** A large portion of the fruit becomes pink; suitable for nearby markets.
5. **Firm red stage:** Fruit is red but still firm; suitable for fresh use and some processing.
6. **Fully ripe stage:** Fruit is fully coloured and soft; suitable for processing or seed extraction.

Harvest gently with the fruit stalk where appropriate. Avoid harvesting wet fruits because they are more likely to develop post-harvest disease.

Frequent picking encourages continued flowering and reduces overripe fruit on the plant.

---

## 20. Sorting, Grading, Packaging, and Storage

Immediately after harvest:

- Remove cracked, rotten, diseased, insect-damaged, and deformed fruits.
- Keep fruits in shade.
- Sort by variety, size, colour, shape, firmness, and maturity.
- Use clean, ventilated plastic crates rather than overfilled sacks or rough baskets.
- Avoid dropping or compressing fruits.
- Separate mature-green fruits for distant markets from pink or red fruits for local sale.
- Do not wash fruits with contaminated water.

For short-term storage and transport, tomatoes should be kept cool but not exposed to chilling temperatures. Fully ripe fruits require more careful handling and have a shorter shelf life than mature-green fruits.

---

## 21. Expected Yield

Yield depends greatly on variety, season, crop duration, plant density, soil fertility, pest pressure, irrigation, and whether the crop is grown in the open field or under protected conditions.

Open-pollinated varieties generally produce less than well-managed hybrids. Plastic-house indeterminate tomatoes can provide a much longer harvest and higher yield than ordinary open-field crops, but they also require greater investment, labour, technical management, and disease control.

Farmers should evaluate success through:

- Marketable yield rather than total fruit weight
- Production cost per kilogram
- Percentage of damaged or rejected fruit
- Selling price across the season
- Labour and input cost
- Duration of harvest
- Profit per ropani or plastic house

Very high theoretical yields should not be used as the main planning target without considering local conditions and production costs.

---

## 22. Seed Production

Tomato is mainly self-pollinated, but varietal purity still requires careful field selection and isolation.

For seed production:

- Use true-to-type, healthy plants.
- Remove off-type and diseased plants.
- Select fully ripe fruits from productive plants.
- Extract seed by fermentation, alkali treatment, or another recommended method.
- Wash seed thoroughly.
- Dry seed in shade, not under excessive heat.
- Store only when seed is properly dried.
- Label the seed with variety, location, and harvest date.

Hybrid seed should not normally be saved for replanting because the next generation may not remain uniform or retain the original performance.

---

## 23. Record Keeping and Farm Planning

Commercial farmers should maintain records of:

- Variety and seed source
- Nursery sowing and transplanting dates
- Fertilizer and manure use
- Irrigation dates
- Pesticide or biological-control applications
- Pest and disease observations
- Harvest quantity by date
- Grading losses
- Selling price and buyer
- Labour and input costs

These records help farmers compare seasons, identify recurring problems, plan market timing, and calculate actual profit.

---

## 24. Practical Production Checklist

Before planting, confirm that:

- The variety matches the location, season, and market.
- Seed comes from a reliable source.
- The nursery is clean and well drained.
- The field has not recently carried a serious wilt or nematode problem.
- Soil drainage and irrigation are available.
- Compost is fully decomposed.
- The spacing matches the plant type and training system.
- Stakes, strings, mulch, and drip materials are ready.
- A crop-rotation and disease-prevention plan is in place.
- The expected harvesting period matches market demand.

During production:

- Inspect the crop at least twice each week.
- Remove infected plants and damaged fruits early.
- Keep irrigation and fertilizer balanced.
- Maintain ventilation in plastic houses.
- Prune and tie plants regularly.
- Use pesticides only after correct diagnosis and according to the approved label.
- Observe the pre-harvest interval before selling fruits.

## Appendix A: Land-Area Units and Accurate Conversions

Nepal commonly uses two traditional land-measurement systems:

- **Hill system:** ropani, aana, paisa, and daam
- **Terai system:** bigha, kattha, and dhur

The values below are the standard units used in Nepal. A bigha used in another country or region may have a different area, so the country must always be identified before converting.

### A.1 Standard land units used in Nepal

| Unit           |         Internal relationship | Square metres (m²) | Square feet (ft²) |
| -------------- | ----------------------------: | -----------------: | ----------------: |
| 1 daam         |            Smallest hill unit |              1.987 |             21.39 |
| 1 paisa        |                        4 daam |              7.949 |             85.56 |
| 1 aana         |             4 paisa = 16 daam |             31.796 |            342.25 |
| 1 ropani       | 16 aana = 64 paisa = 256 daam |            508.737 |             5,476 |
| 1 dhur         |           Smallest Terai unit |             16.932 |            182.25 |
| 1 kattha       |                       20 dhur |            338.631 |             3,645 |
| 1 bigha        |          20 kattha = 400 dhur |           6,772.63 |            72,900 |
| 1 acre         |            International unit |          4,046.856 |            43,560 |
| 1 hectare (ha) |                     10,000 m² |             10,000 |           107,639 |

Minor differences in the final decimal may occur because some published tables round the square-foot or square-metre value.

### A.2 Direct comparison of common units

| Given area | Equivalent area |
| ---------- | --------------: |
| 1 ropani   | 0.05087 hectare |
| 1 ropani   |    0.12568 acre |
| 1 ropani   |    1.502 kattha |
| 1 ropani   |   0.07512 bigha |
| 1 kattha   |  0.66575 ropani |
| 1 bigha    |   13.312 ropani |
| 1 acre     |    7.958 ropani |
| 1 acre     |   11.949 kattha |
| 1 acre     |    0.5975 bigha |
| 1 hectare  |   19.657 ropani |
| 1 hectare  |   29.531 kattha |
| 1 hectare  |    1.4763 bigha |

### A.3 Conversion formulas

Use square metres as the common base unit when converting between systems.

```text
square metres = ropani × 508.737
square metres = bigha × 6,772.63
square metres = kattha × 338.631
square metres = hectare × 10,000

ropani = square metres ÷ 508.737
bigha = square metres ÷ 6,772.63
kattha = square metres ÷ 338.631
hectare = square metres ÷ 10,000
```

For a rectangular plot:

```text
area in m² = length in metres × width in metres
```

For a triangular plot:

```text
area in m² = (base in metres × perpendicular height in metres) ÷ 2
```

For an irregular plot, divide it into smaller rectangles and triangles, calculate each area, and add the results. A cadastral survey should be used when a legally precise measurement is required.

### A.4 Worked examples

**Example 1: Convert 3 ropani into square metres**

```text
3 × 508.737 = 1,526.211 m²
```

Therefore, **3 ropani is approximately 1,526.21 m²**.

**Example 2: Convert 2 bigha 5 kattha into square metres and ropani**

```text
2 bigha = 2 × 6,772.63 = 13,545.26 m²
5 kattha = 5 × 338.631 = 1,693.155 m²
total = 13,545.26 + 1,693.155 = 15,238.415 m²
ropani = 15,238.415 ÷ 508.737 = 29.95 ropani
```

Therefore, **2 bigha 5 kattha is approximately 15,238.42 m² or 29.95 ropani**.

**Example 3: Convert 1 ropani 8 aana into square metres**

```text
1 ropani = 508.737 m²
8 aana = 8 × 31.796 = 254.368 m²
total = 508.737 + 254.368 = 763.105 m²
```

Therefore, **1 ropani 8 aana is approximately 763.11 m²**.

**Example 4: Calculate plant population from land area and spacing**

At a spacing of 75 cm × 60 cm:

```text
75 cm = 0.75 m
60 cm = 0.60 m
area required per plant = 0.75 × 0.60 = 0.45 m²
theoretical plants per ropani = 508.737 ÷ 0.45 = 1,130.53
```

The theoretical population is about **1,131 plants per ropani**. The actual number will be lower after allowing space for paths, drains, borders, irrigation lines, and the plastic-house structure.

**Example 5: Convert a recommendation given per ropani to one hectare**

If the fertilizer recommendation is 16 kg DAP per ropani:

```text
1 hectare = 19.657 ropani
DAP per hectare = 16 × 19.657 = 314.512 kg
```

Therefore, the equivalent is approximately **314.5 kg DAP per hectare**. This is only a unit conversion; the agronomic recommendation should still be checked against soil-test results and local guidance.

### A.5 Rules for interpreting land-unit questions

- Treat **m²** as square metres, not linear metres.
- A metre measures length; a square metre measures area.
- Do not convert bigha without confirming the country or regional standard.
- If the context is Nepal, use **1 bigha = 6,772.63 m²** unless another definition is explicitly stated.
- Preserve mixed units before conversion. For example, calculate “2 bigha 5 kattha” as two separate parts and then add them.
- Clearly label rounded answers as approximate.
- For input, pesticide, seed, fertilizer, yield, or cost calculations, first convert the full land area into a single unit.
- Unit conversion does not by itself confirm that an agricultural recommendation is suitable.

## Appendix B: Technical-Term Glossary
